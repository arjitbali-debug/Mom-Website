Biodynamic Craniosacral Therapy

Gentle touch, careful listening and time for stillness. Learn about Meeta’s approach and what a session may involve.

A gentle, attentive approach

Biodynamic Craniosacral Therapy (BCST) is a complementary hands-on practice using light, still touch. Practitioners describe the biodynamic approach as listening to the body and its inherent capacity for health, rather than working through forceful manipulation.[1]

For Meeta, stillness and balance are central to the way she approaches a session. She aims to offer a calm setting, respect your comfort and allow the work to proceed at a pace you can discuss with her.

Her interest in the body and nervous system is part of her practice philosophy. It is not a promise that a session will correct a particular condition or produce a measurable change in nervous-system function.

What might a session involve?

The following describes the usual BCST session format outlined by BCTA/NA. Meeta can explain how it would be adapted for you.[3]

  1. A conversation first

    Discuss what brings you, relevant health history and what you hope to explore. This is also a chance to ask about touch and comfort.

  2. Getting comfortable

    You usually remain fully clothed and lie supported on a treatment table. Ask for adjustments to positioning, lighting or temperature.

  3. Light, still contact

    Contact may be at the feet, head, sacrum or another agreed area. The practitioner remains attentive to your experience and checks that the contact feels comfortable.

  4. Time to reflect

    Afterwards, there is time to discuss what you noticed. Experiences vary; there is no particular sensation you need to have.

You can ask to change or stop the contact at any time. Confirm session duration and suitability directly with Meeta.

Understanding the craniosacral system

What is cerebrospinal fluid?

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is the clear fluid in the brain’s ventricles and around the brain and spinal cord. It helps cushion these structures and supports their chemical environment and waste transport. Much of it is produced by the choroid plexus, a specialized tissue inside the ventricles.[4]

“Cranial” refers to the skull; the sacrum is the bone at the base of the spine. Craniosacral practitioners use these anatomical terms when describing their approach, but the anatomy alone does not demonstrate a treatment mechanism.

Does this fluid cross the blood–brain barrier?

There are two distinct protective interfaces: the blood–brain barrier at brain blood vessels, and the blood–CSF barrier at the choroid plexus. They regulate exchanges between blood and the nervous system. CSF is produced and circulated through regulated processes; describing it simply as fluid that “crosses the blood–brain barrier” is misleading.[5]

Does the practitioner guide or push the fluid?

BCST practitioners describe listening through touch. This should not be understood as physically pushing CSF through the brain or moving it across its protective barriers. The session description explains the practice without claiming that touch controls CSF circulation.

What does “balance” mean in Meeta’s approach?

It describes her intention to offer stillness, comfort and space for a person to feel more at ease. It is not a claim that sympathetic and parasympathetic activity will be brought into a fixed balance or that a medical condition will be corrected.

BCST is a complementary practice and does not replace medical assessment, educational support or mental-health care.
